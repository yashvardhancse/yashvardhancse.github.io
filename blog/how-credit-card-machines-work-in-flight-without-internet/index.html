How Credit Card Machines Work in Flight (With No Internet)

Card machines on planes and in shops can keep taking payments with no signal when they are set up for it. Here is which part of a payment needs a network, how the chip and terminal decide offline, and who carries the risk when the bank is asked later.

Key takeaways

  • Credit card machines work without internet when offline mode is set up: a card payment is two links, and only the one to the bank needs a network.
  • A chip’s offline approval follows issuer-loaded limits, while a store-and-forward “approved” is the terminal’s own decision; vendors put a later decline on the seller.
  • Trade press reports that many airlines use point-of-sale systems with an offline mode; no source cited here gives a current share.
  • PCI DSS (the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) bars keeping the card verification code or PIN block after authorization, even encrypted.

A card machine with no internet can read the chip, check the card is genuine, verify an offline PIN and get a cryptogram, because that happens between card and machine. It cannot ask the issuing bank, so its settings decide: decline, approve a small sale under preset limits, or store the sale and ask later, as some airline handhelds do.

How do credit card machines work without internet? The two-link model

Every card payment is two conversations. In the first, the card and terminal talk directly, through the chip contacts or over NFC. In the second, the terminal asks the cardholder’s bank (the issuer), through the merchant’s payment institution (the acquirer) and the card network, such as Visa, whether the money can move. Only the second needs a network (Figure 1).

A chip card is a small computer: its issuer loads keys, a transaction counter and risk rules, so on Link A it can compute a one-time code, a cryptogram, without asking anyone. Link B turns a sale into money, and on some flights it is missing (see the in-flight section below).

What can a card machine check with no network? (link one)

Chip and terminal can prove the card is genuine, check a PIN the chip can verify and produce a cryptogram. All of it is specified by EMV, the chip standard published by EMVCo.[1]

Is the card genuine? Offline data authentication

Static data authentication (SDA) checks a signature over fixed data, so the data can be copied. A 2010 paper describes the resulting “yes card”, a counterfeit that answers yes to any PIN at a terminal with no online link; most UK terminals were permanently online and could normally detect it.[2] Dynamic data authentication (DDA) and its combined form with cryptogram generation (CDA) resist this: the card signs a number supplied by the terminal with a private key.[1][2] Visa now requires DDA, not SDA, on non-contactless chip cards that support offline authorization (rule 4.1.17.28).[3]

Is it the cardholder? Offline PIN, signature or nothing

The issuer puts an ordered list of verification methods on the card, and the terminal works down it (section 10.5).[1] An offline PIN is checked by the chip and needs no link; an online PIN goes encrypted to the issuer and does.

What is a cryptogram? TC, ARQC and AAC

The terminal asks the chip for a TC (transaction certificate: approve offline), an ARQC (authorization request cryptogram: ask the issuer) or an AAC (application authentication cryptogram: decline).[1] Each is a one-time code computed from the transaction data and a secret key; the issuer holds the key behind an ARQC. The chip never moves money: a TC is its approval under the rules loaded into it, and an ARQC hands the decision to the issuer.

What needs a network: authorization, clearing and settlement (link two)

Everything that moves money. Visa’s glossary splits it into three jobs. Rule numbers here are Visa’s; other networks publish their own.[3]

Authorization
Approval by the issuer, a processor or stand-in processing; Visa counts offline authorization too.
Clearing
Checking and valuing the transaction records that members exchange.
Settlement
Reporting and moving the money owed once clearing is done.

Online authorization lets the issuer review sales outside acceptable risk limits and check the cryptogram (Book 3, section 10.9).[1] Offline, none of that happens at the counter.

How does a terminal decide to approve a payment offline?

The terminal compares risk flags with masks from the acquirer and the issuer, then asks the chip for a TC, ARQC or AAC (Figure 2). This models the contact-chip flow in EMV Books 3 and 4 with invented masks, not any airline’s terminals; contactless kernels and acquirer settings differ.

  1. Risk checks set flags. Floor-limit checking (a floor limit is the amount that decides when a sale must go online) can add a recent logged sale on the same card, to catch split sales. Floor limits are not one global number: Visa’s public rules omit the values, and acquirers configure them (with Adyen, a seller gives Support the limits it wants).[3][4] Random selection sends some small sales online, and velocity checking compares the card’s counter with its value at the last online approval. Failures set bits in the five-byte Terminal Verification Results (TVR), as do earlier steps such as data authentication (Book 3, section 10.6).[1]
  2. Action codes map flags to actions. The acquirer’s Terminal Action Codes and the issuer’s Issuer Action Codes each come as Denial, Online and Default masks, checked in that order. A Denial match asks for an AAC; an Online match, on a terminal that can go online, asks for an ARQC; otherwise, on such a terminal, it asks for a TC (section 10.7).[1]
  3. The card gets a vote. A TC request is only a proposal: the chip’s own risk management may answer ARQC or AAC (sections 10.7 and 10.8). Cards built to EMV’s Common Core Definitions count their offline approvals and amounts against issuer-set limits; past the lower limit the card asks for online approval, and past the upper limit it normally declines if the terminal cannot go online.[1]
  4. If the link is down. An Online match means the terminal wanted an ARQC and cannot send it, so it checks the Default masks: a match asks the chip to decline (response code Z3), no match to approve (Y3). The chip then answers TC or AAC (Book 4, section 12.2.1).[1]

Offline EMV approval vs store-and-forward: what is the difference?

In offline EMV approval, the kind Table 1 shows, the chip is in control: the terminal checks the PIN against the chip and asks the card to approve, and the issuer’s configuration of the card decides.[4] Visa calls this offline authorization and describes it as issuer-controlled.[3] In store-and-forward, often sold as offline credit card processing or offline mode, the terminal or app approves the sale itself and asks the issuer only after the device reconnects.[4][5]

Store-and-forward is a separate mode with its own limits (a maximum amount and number of payments per terminal). Adyen says it approves without verification, so an issuer decline is likelier than with offline EMV, which can go straight into clearing but works with most major credit card schemes and not most debit cards.[4] Swipes are the weak case: they carry no cryptogram, and Stripe and Adyen block them offline.[4][5]

How do airlines take card payments in flight?

Public sources describe two designs: an offline handheld that sends sales for authorization after landing, and live processing over a satellite link (Table 2). Runway Girl Network reports that many airlines use point-of-sale systems with an offline mode for low-value sales; no source cited here gives a current share.[6] Live processing needs no passenger Wi-Fi: in a flight test reported in 2022, the fflya system used Iridium satellites and no broadband passenger connection.[6]

PAX describes one airline (unnamed) whose Android handheld took card payments offline in flight, sent them to the acquiring bank after landing, and batched rejected ones hourly for review and resubmission.[7] Finnair’s 2021 notice warned of occasional card-payment disruptions on flights “with no online connection with banks”.[8]

Finnair’s help page, an answer about two-step authentication, says contactless payments up to EUR 50 and credit cards up to EUR 800 work onboard, and that the terminal occasionally asks for the card to be inserted and a PIN entered. EUR 50 also matches the per-payment limit in the EU’s 2018 contactless exemption from strong customer authentication;[9][10] the page does not say who set either figure or whether either is an offline limit.

Tip: If you pay in flight

On a flight with no link, the receipt shows the terminal’s decision, not your bank’s. With store-and-forward the bank is asked after the device reconnects, so a charge can post after you have left the aircraft.[7]

Store-and-forward step by step: from tap to statement

Store-and-forward means taking the sale now and asking for authorization later. Five steps follow one sale from gate to statement (Figure 3); this generic model comes from vendor documents and Visa’s rules, not an airline’s procedure.

  1. Before boarding. Typically, per vendor documents for ground devices, the device is online, syncs software and receives its limits: a maximum per sale, a cap on offline sales held, a time window.[4][5]
  2. At the tap. The terminal runs the checks above and, if the sale passes, stores the record and updates a running count and total.
  3. After landing. The device uploads its queue and the acquirer sends each sale to the issuer as a flagged deferred authorization. Under Visa’s published rules the request is due within 24 hours of the transaction date for most merchant categories (rule 5.7.3.3), or for in-transit sales within 24 hours of the vehicle reaching its final destination (rule 5.7.3.4); the text lists these as separate cases and does not say which covers an airline’s in-flight sales.[3]
  4. The issuer decides. An approval makes it an ordinary authorized sale; a decline lands on the seller under vendor terms, covered below.
  5. Clearing and settlement. Visa’s general limit for the acquirer to clear a card-present sale is five calendar days from a valid authorization (Visa’s Table 5-11), with country exceptions in Table 5-12; in Europe, an intraregional contactless sale must reach clearing within two calendar days of the transaction date (rule 5.7.3.5). Issuers can dispute declined-authorization and no-authorization sales for 75 days from processing (conditions 11.2 and 11.3).[3]

Example: Worked example: a 6.50 coffee

Say you tap for a 6.50 coffee at 14:05 on a flight whose 17:35 landing is the aircraft’s final destination, and the handheld has no link. Under Visa’s in-transit rule the seller must obtain authorization by 17:35 the next day, 27.5 hours after the tap; under the deferred-authorization rule the 24 hours would run from the transaction date, which is earlier. If the issuer declines, no charge posts for that attempt, and under the vendor terms below the seller carries the loss.

Who pays if an offline card payment is declined later?

Usually the seller. Square, Stripe and Adyen make the seller responsible for a later decline, and Clover says the merchant accepts that risk.[4][5][12][13] These are shop-terminal vendors. No airline or in-flight vendor page cited here says what happens after a declined in-flight sale, apart from Runway Girl Network’s mention of block lists and collection services and PAX’s hourly resubmission of rejected transactions.[6][7]

Card-network rules (Visa’s here; other networks differ) govern acquirer and issuer. The seller’s contract with its acquirer governs what the seller owes (Table 3).

Does contactless payment work without internet?

The tap can work with no network; the approval depends on the terminal. Apple’s security guide says a phone wallet builds a one-time cryptogram on the device and makes a payment only after the Secure Enclave authorizes it, so the check on you happens on the phone; it does not discuss airplane mode.[15] An aviation-payments vendor said in December 2025 that Apple Pay and Google Pay payments in flight are possible but inconsistent, because wallet tokens often need the phone online to refresh and only some issuers support offline tokens.[16]

The terminal’s offline settings then decide. Stripe does not support offline taps where Strong Customer Authentication applies, and Square’s Tap to Pay does not work offline.[5][12] For a physical card, an online PIN cannot be checked offline.

What does PCI DSS say about card data stored offline?

Sensitive authentication data (SAD) means full track data, the card verification code and the PIN or PIN block. PCI DSS Requirement 3.3.1 prohibits storing SAD after authorization, even if encrypted.[17][18] PCI’s FAQ 1154 (July 2025) adds that there is no time limit for holding SAD before authorization, but it must be protected, and that the card brands decide whether it may be stored then at all; several prohibit it outright.[17]

While a sale waits, Requirement 3.3.2 says SAD stored electronically must be strongly encrypted. A note under Requirement 3.3.1.1 says the cardholder name, primary account number (PAN), expiry date and service code may be retained if needed and stored securely.[18] Numbering follows PCI DSS v4.0, as in the questionnaire cited and the July 2025 FAQ; check the current version.

Where else does offline card acceptance matter?

Anywhere a card machine can lose its link: Visa’s in-transit definition covers ships, boats, buses, aircraft, ferries and trains,[3] and Stripe and Clover document offline modes for shops.[5][13] In Europe, offline acceptance is becoming a design goal. The Paypers reported in October 2026 that all newly issued Mastercard cards in Europe will support offline payments from February 2027 and new and replacement terminals by May 2027, with local approval up to EUR 200 per transaction under bank-set limits stored on the chip.[19] Switzerland’s Federal Office for National Economic Supply described, on 24 August 2026, a nationwide scheme in which card and terminal authorize directly, with broad deployment planned by the end of 2027.[20]

Myths about card machines without internet

Myth: a chip’s offline approval means the bank approved the payment

Offline, the chip returns a TC, its approval under limits the issuer loaded and the terminal’s settings allow (Book 3, section 10.8).[1] The issuer sees the sale only later, in clearing. Online, the issuer approves by checking an ARQC. Either way the chip never moves money.

Myth: every in-flight terminal calls the bank over the plane’s Wi-Fi

Some do not. Vendor documents describe authorization after landing, Finnair’s 2021 notice acknowledged flights with no online connection with banks, and Runway Girl Network says many airlines use an offline mode.[6][7][8][11]

Frequently asked questions

How do credit card machines work without internet?

A card payment has two links. The chip-to-machine link is local and works with no network: the chip can prove it is genuine, check a PIN if it supports offline PIN, and produce a cryptogram. The machine-to-bank link needs a network, so with none the machine declines the sale, lets the chip and preset limits approve a small one offline, or records the sale and asks for authorization later (store-and-forward), where the seller carries the decline risk.

Can you use a credit card on a plane without Wi-Fi?

It depends on the airline and the aircraft. Runway Girl Network reports that many airlines use point-of-sale systems with an offline mode for low-value sales, and live processing over a satellite link has been tested. Finnair’s help page says contactless payments up to EUR 50 and Mastercard, Visa and American Express credit cards up to EUR 800 are accepted onboard, and that the terminal occasionally asks for the card to be inserted and a PIN entered. Debit cards may behave differently, since Adyen says most debit cards do not support offline EMV approval, so check the airline’s current page before you fly.

How do airlines process credit card payments in flight?

Public sources describe two designs: in store-and-forward, a handheld approves the sale under its limits and authorization is requested after landing, and in live processing the request goes over a satellite link, which has been shown in tests. No source cited here gives a current share of airlines using each. Under Visa’s published rules, an in-transit sale needs authorization within 24 hours of the vehicle reaching its final destination.

Does contactless payment work without internet?

The tap itself can: a phone wallet builds a one-time cryptogram on the device, as Apple’s security guide describes, and the phone checks you on the device too. Whether the sale is approved with no network depends on the terminal’s offline settings and limits, and an online PIN cannot be checked. An aviation-payments vendor said in December 2025 that wallet payments in flight are possible but inconsistent, and Apple’s security guide does not say how Apple Pay behaves in airplane mode.

What happens if a card is declined after an offline payment?

In store-and-forward the decline appears only when the sale is uploaded for authorization. Square, Stripe and Adyen say the seller carries that risk, Clover says the merchant accepts it, and Stripe says the funds may not be recoverable. If the chip itself approved the sale offline under issuer-loaded limits, Visa’s rules treat it differently, but the seller’s contract can still leave it liable for disputes, as Adyen’s terms do.

Can I be charged after I leave the plane or shop?

Yes. A charge can post after you have left, because with store-and-forward the bank is asked only once the device reconnects; vendor windows run from 72 hours to upload at Square and 7 days of offline operation at Clover to a month of retries at Adyen and 30 days of resending in Adyen’s Immfly case study, but no source states a maximum delay for an airline. If the issuer declines the delayed request, that attempt is not charged, though retries are possible: Adyen retries eligible failed payments for a month, and Visa lets merchants reattempt many declines up to 20 times in 30 days. Square, Stripe and Adyen put that loss on the seller.

What is a floor limit?

Visa defines it as an amount, set by Visa, above which online authorization is required. A chip terminal compares the sale amount, plus recent sales on the same card where the terminal keeps a log, with its configured floor limit, and reaching it sets a flag that normally forces an online request. There is no single global figure: it varies by network, country, contact or contactless, and acquirer settings.

Is the PIN checked offline?

Sometimes. The card’s issuer-set verification list decides: an offline PIN is checked by the chip, an online PIN goes to the issuer and needs a connection, and signature or no verification also exist. Adyen says its mobile store-and-forward mode supports offline PIN only. Finnair says its terminal occasionally asks for the card to be inserted and a PIN entered, which it ties to confirming the cardholder.
