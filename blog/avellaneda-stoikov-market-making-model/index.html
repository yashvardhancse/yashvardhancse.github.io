Avellaneda-Stoikov Model Explained (With Python Code)

How the Avellaneda-Stoikov model tells a market maker where to quote when inventory is risky: every symbol defined, the formulas sketched from the HJB equation, how accurate they are, and a short NumPy simulation you can run.

Key takeaways

  • A market maker who is long wants to sell, so the model shifts both quotes down in proportion to inventory (the reservation price) and sets the spread from risk aversion, volatility, time left and fill-rate decay.
  • The closed form is approximate. Guéant, Lehalle and Fernandez-Tapia note that it is a Taylor expansion of the exact quotes near the horizon; a check here finds it shifts quotes too far when much time remains and fills are frequent.
  • In a 1,000-path simulation at γ = 0.1 (script below), inventory-aware quoting halves the standard deviation of profit and loss (P&L), 6.5 against 12.9, but earns slightly less (65.1 against 69.0): lower risk, not higher profit.
  • The model ignores adverse selection, fees, tick size and queue priority: a framework for inventory risk, not a ready-made strategy.

The Avellaneda-Stoikov model (2008) tells a market maker where to quote when inventory is risky. It centers the bid and ask on a reservation price, r = s − qγσ²(T − t), below the mid-price s when the market maker is long q shares, and sets a total spread of γσ²(T − t) + (2/γ)ln(1 + γ/k).

What problem does the Avellaneda-Stoikov model solve?

It tells a market maker (the paper's "dealer") where to place a bid and an ask around the mid-price, the halfway point between the best bid and best ask, when holding inventory is risky. Each fill earns part of the spread but changes the position: a dealer bidding 99.50 around a price of 100 that buys 5 shares earns 2.50 against the mid-price, but if the price then falls by 2 those shares lose 10. Avellaneda and Stoikov (A-S) framed this as a control problem.[1]

Avellaneda-Stoikov model assumptions and symbols

The 2008 paper's setup:[1]

  • Price. The mid-price follows dS = σdW, with W a Brownian motion (a continuous random walk): constant volatility σ and no drift.
  • Quotes. The dealer posts a one-share bid at s − δb and ask at s + δa, updated continuously at no cost.
  • Fills. Market sells hit the bid and market buys lift the ask as Poisson processes (random events at a steady average rate). A quote at distance δ from the mid-price fills at rate λ(δ) = Ae−kδ.
  • State. The dealer holds q shares (negative means short) and cash X. There are no fees, tick size or inventory limit.
  • Objective. Maximize the expected exponential utility of final wealth (a payoff that penalizes risk, scaled by γ), E[−exp(−γ(XT + qTST))], with leftover inventory valued at the final mid-price.
Symbols and units. σ is volatility, so the formulas contain σ².

Avellaneda-Stoikov reservation price: how inventory moves your quotes

The reservation price is the dealer's inventory-adjusted fair value: the mid-price minus the marginal risk cost of the shares already held.[1]

r(s,q,t)=s−qγσ2(T−t)

Equation 1: Reservation price

In words: mid-price minus inventory times risk aversion times variance rate times time left. A long dealer (q > 0) values the asset below the mid-price and prefers to sell; a short dealer (q < 0) values it above and prefers to buy. The shift vanishes at the horizon, where leftover inventory is marked at the mid-price. Traders call this shift of both quotes the skew.

Holding q shares until T exposes final wealth to a variance of q²σ²(T − t), which an exponential-utility dealer prices at about ½γσ²(T − t)q²; one more share adds the slope, proportional to q. For a dealer who stops trading, the paper's "frozen inventory" case, this is exact: Equation 1 is the average of the indifference prices for buying one more share and for selling one, which differ by γσ2(T − t).

Take s = 100, γ = 0.1, σ = 2, k = 1.5 and T − t = 1. Each share shifts the price by 0.1 × 2² × 1 = 0.4, so a dealer long 5 shares values the asset at 98.00 and one short 5 shares at 102.00 (Figure 1).

Avellaneda-Stoikov optimal spread formula

The paper's closed-form (approximate) spread is a risk term plus a fill-rate term, centered on the reservation price.[1]

δa+δb=γσ2(T−t)+2γln(1+γk)

Equation 2: Total spread (closed form)

The bid is r minus half the spread and the ask is r plus half. Measured from the mid-price, and writing L for the per-side fill-rate term (1/γ)ln(1 + γ/k), the offsets are:

δb=L+1+2q2γσ2(T−t)δa=L+1−2q2γσ2(T−t)

Equation 3: Bid and ask offsets from the mid-price

In words: each offset is L plus a share of the risk term that depends on inventory, so a long dealer (q > 0) bids further below the mid-price and asks closer to it.

  • Risk term. γσ2(T − t) is the gap between the indifference prices for selling and for buying one share. It shrinks to zero at the horizon.
  • Fill-rate term. (2/γ)ln(1 + γ/k) trades margin against fill probability. As γ → 0 it tends to 2/k, the risk-neutral choice: a half-spread of 1/k maximizes the profit rate δ·λ(δ). It is also the spread's floor at the horizon: 1.29 for γ = 0.1 and k = 1.5.

In this closed form q does not appear in the spread (the exact spread varies only slightly with q), so inventory moves the quotes without widening them.

Warning: Check the spread formula

At least one public student report prints the fill-rate term as ln(1 + γ/k) without the factor 2/γ, a twentyfold understatement at γ = 0.1.[2] Check against equation (30) of the paper.[1]

Pick γ by simulating the inventory and P&L spread you can tolerate. The paper tries 0.01 (close to risk neutral), 0.1, and 1 (very risk averse) at a price of 100.[1] Its unit is 1/price, so values rarely transfer between assets. Figure 2 plots the spread against γ.

Derivation sketch: from the HJB equation to the closed form

A sketch of the paper's argument, not a proof: it skips boundary conditions, regularity and verification.[1] Let u(s, x, q, t) be the best expected utility reachable from price s, cash x and inventory q at time t. Dynamic programming gives the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation below, with u = −exp(−γ(x + qs)) at t = T: the price diffuses, and each max term is one side's fill, at rate λ, which moves inventory by one share and cash by the fill price.

0=ut+12σ2uss+maxδbλ(δb)×[u(s,x−s+δb,q+1,t)−u]+maxδaλ(δa)×[u(s,x+s+δa,q−1,t)−u]

Equation 4: The HJB equation of the market-making problem

  1. Remove cash. The ansatz (a trial form) u = −exp(−γx)·exp(−γθ(s, q, t)) separates cash out and leaves an equation for θ with θ = qs at T.
  2. Place the quotes. The indifference prices are differences of θ: rb = θ(q + 1) − θ(q) and ra = θ(q) − θ(q − 1). With fill rate Ae−kδ, each first-order condition puts the bid (1/γ)ln(1 + γ/k) below rb and the ask the same distance above ra: the fill-rate term of Equation 2.
  3. Expand and linearize. The remaining equation is nonlinear, so the paper expands θ in powers of q and linearizes the exponential fill terms. The terms of order q and q² give Equations 1 and 2. The linearized fill term is what makes the reservation price equal the frozen-inventory one.

Note: Exact or approximate?

Exact: the frozen-inventory prices, the HJB equation and steps 1 and 2. Approximate, for a dealer who keeps quoting: Equations 1 and 2, because step 3 truncates the expansion and linearizes the fills, as the paper itself says.[1]

Avellaneda-Stoikov in Python: a runnable simulation

The script below repeats the paper's experiment in 55 lines of NumPy.[1] Two dealers see the same 1,000 random price paths: a symmetric dealer centered on the mid-price, and one that follows Equations 1 and 2. As in the paper, the symmetric dealer uses the inventory-aware dealer's average spread. In each 0.005 step a quote fills with probability λ(δ) times the step, capped at 1. Run pip install numpy, save the code as simulate.py and execute it. The output is from Python 3.13.7 and NumPy 2.2.6 with seed 42; other seeds, versions or platforms change the digits but not the pattern.[3]

At γ = 0.1 the inventory-aware dealer earned 65.1 against 69.0 for the symmetric dealer (Figure 3), with a P&L standard deviation of 6.5 against 12.9 and a final-inventory standard deviation of 2.98 shares against 8.54. The paper's Table 1 gives 65.0, 6.6 and 2.9 against 68.4, 12.7 and 8.4, close to the script's. The closed-form dealer earns slightly less at γ = 0.01 (68.8 against 69.5) and much less at γ = 1 (31.4 against 43.7), each with lower risk; much of the γ = 1 gap is the closed form's own error (next section). P&L is in price units for an asset at 100, not a return.

Myth: the model makes more money

Here, even with the exact quotes below, it earns slightly less than symmetric quoting at γ = 0.1. Across seeds 100 to 119 (1,000 paths each), the inventory-aware dealer had a lower mean P&L in 20 of 20 runs and a lower P&L standard deviation in 20 of 20. Judged by certainty equivalent, CE = −ln(mean(exp(−γ·P&L)))/γ, the guaranteed amount a γ = 0.1 investor would accept instead of the P&L distribution, it wins in 20 of 20 runs of 20,000 paths: 62.7 to 62.8 against 42 to 57 for symmetric quoting, whose figure swings because a few very bad paths dominate it.

Is the Avellaneda-Stoikov formula exact? GLFT and later models

No. Guéant, Lehalle and Fernandez-Tapia (GLFT) note in a footnote that the quotes from Equations 1 and 2 are the Taylor expansion of the exact optimal quotes for times close to T.[4] Their exact solution keeps the model and adds an inventory limit.

The exact quotes follow GLFT's Theorem 1. With α = kγσ²/2 and η = A(1 + γ/k)−(1 + k/γ), let M be the tridiagonal matrix with diagonal αq² and off-diagonal −η for |q| ≤ 30, and v = exp(−M(T − t))·(1, …, 1). Then δb = (1/k)ln(vq/vq+1) + (1/γ)ln(1 + γ/k), δa is the same with vq−1, and the skew per share is (δb − δa)/(2q), read at q = 2.

The two agree near the horizon (ratio 1.05 at T − t = 0.05), but at the full horizon the closed form moves quotes 7.6 times as far per share, while the exact skew levels off near 0.052. That plateau matches GLFT's long-run closed form, √(σ²γ/(2kA) × (1 + γ/k)1 + k/γ), which needs no terminal time.[4] The ratio depends on A, which the closed form ignores: for A = 0.14, 1.4, 14, 140 and 1,400, the closed-form skew at T − t = 1 is 1.0, 1.2, 2.4, 7.6 and 24 times the exact skew. The closed form prices inventory held until T; frequent fills unwind it sooner.

The exact solution also responds differently: doubling σ doubles the skew, doubling γ raises it about 1.4 times, doubling A or k lowers it by about 30%, and the spread falls slightly as γ rises. In the simulation (seed 100, 20,000 paths, computed for this article) the closed-form spread at γ = 1, q = 0 and T − t = 1 is 5.02 against the exact 1.22, and the closed-form dealer earns 31.5 against 64.0 for the exact quotes. At γ = 0.1 the exact quotes earn 67.9 (standard deviation 7.3) against 68.3 (13.4) for symmetric quoting: still slightly less profit, with about half the risk.

Cartea-Jaimungal and other extensions

Cartea, Jaimungal and co-authors replace exponential utility with a running penalty on squared inventory and add adverse selection, short-term price predictors and self-exciting order flow.[5] Guéant (2017) reconciles both formulations.[6]

How to estimate k (kappa) and A from market data

For σ, divide the standard deviation of recent mid-price changes by the square root of the time step. For k and A, count how often market trades reached each depth per unit time and fit ln λ = ln A − kδ by least squares: slope −k, intercept ln A.[7] Fits are fragile: one public tutorial, fitting ten minutes of ETHUSDT data, got k ≈ 0.018 per tick over the full depth range and 0.042 over the nearest 70 ticks, ignoring queue position when measuring fill rates.[7]

Avellaneda-Stoikov limitations: what the model leaves out

  • Adverse selection. The paper sets aside the risk from informed traders.[1] A dealer can be filled at the bid just before the price falls, the winner's curse, as a 2025 preprint (revised 2026) calls it.[8]
  • Fees, rebates, ticks and queues. A public GLFT backtest tutorial assumes a 0.005% maker rebate and says its five-day profits come from rebates earned by posting quotes at high volume.[7] Guéant notes that A-S-style models can hardly be applied to most stock markets, where prices are discrete and order books are priority queues.[6]
  • Constant parameters. The model fixes σ, A and k over the horizon; one public tutorial refits them every 5 seconds.[7]

Warning: Units differ between implementations

As of October 2026, the docs of Hummingbot's Avellaneda strategy (a V1 strategy its Foundation no longer actively maintains) call its risk_factor unit-less, with common values between 1 and 20: not the paper's γ = 0.1 in units of 1/price. Check units before copying parameters.[9]

Frequently asked questions

What is the Avellaneda-Stoikov model?

It is a 2008 stochastic-control model of market making by Marco Avellaneda and Sasha Stoikov, published in Quantitative Finance. A market maker (the paper's dealer) posts one-share bid and ask limit orders around a Brownian mid-price, fills arrive at a rate that falls exponentially with distance, and the dealer maximizes expected exponential utility of final wealth. The approximate closed-form solution quotes around the reservation price r = s − qγσ²(T − t) with a total spread of γσ²(T − t) + (2/γ)ln(1 + γ/k).

What is the reservation price in the Avellaneda-Stoikov model?

It is the market maker's inventory-adjusted fair value: the mid-price minus the marginal risk cost of the shares held, r = s − qγσ²(T − t). A long market maker (q > 0) has a reservation price below the mid-price and a short one (q < 0) has one above it, and the gap closes as t approaches T. It is exact for a dealer who stops trading and approximate for one who keeps quoting.

How is the Avellaneda-Stoikov optimal spread calculated?

The total spread is γσ²(T − t) + (2/γ)ln(1 + γ/k), centered on the reservation price. The first term is the inventory-risk gap and falls to zero at the horizon. The second balances margin against fill probability and tends to 2/k as γ → 0. In this closed form the spread does not depend on inventory, so the skew comes entirely from the reservation price.

What do gamma, sigma, kappa (k) and A mean, and how do I choose gamma?

γ is risk aversion (units of 1/price), σ is price volatility per square root of time, k (also written κ, kappa) is how fast the fill rate decays with distance from the mid-price (1/price) and A is the fill rate at the mid-price (1/time). A does not appear in the closed-form quotes. γ is a preference rather than a measurement, so choose it by simulating the inventory and P&L spread you can tolerate; the paper tries 0.01, 0.1 and 1 at a price of 100 with σ = 2.

Is the Avellaneda-Stoikov formula exact?

No. It comes from a truncated expansion in inventory plus a linearized fill rate. Guéant, Lehalle and Fernandez-Tapia state that it is the Taylor expansion of the exact quotes near the horizon. In a numerical check computed for this article (γ = 0.1, σ = 2, k = 1.5, A = 140, inventory limit 30), the closed form moved the quotes 7.6 times as far per share as the exact solution at T − t = 1, and 1.05 times as far at T − t = 0.05. The factor depends strongly on A.

How do I estimate k (kappa) and A from market data?

Record how far from the mid-price market trades occurred, count the fills each depth would have received per unit time, and fit ln(rate) = ln A − kδ by least squares: the slope is −k and the intercept is ln A. The result depends strongly on the depth range. One public tutorial, fitting a ten-minute window of ETHUSDT data, found k ≈ 0.018 per tick over the full range and 0.042 over the nearest 70 ticks, ignored queue position when measuring fill rates and refitted every 5 seconds.

Does the Avellaneda-Stoikov model make money in real markets?

The model gives no such guarantee. It leaves out adverse selection, fees and rebates, tick size and queue priority. In the paper and in the simulation here, inventory-aware quoting lowers risk rather than raising profit: at γ = 0.1 the mean P&L is 65.1 against 69.0 for symmetric quoting, with about half the standard deviation. This is education, not advice.

How does it differ from Guéant-Lehalle-Fernandez-Tapia and Cartea-Jaimungal?

GLFT keep the Avellaneda-Stoikov model, add an inventory limit and solve it exactly with linear differential equations, with closed-form long-run approximations. Cartea and Jaimungal replace exponential utility with a running penalty on squared inventory and add adverse selection, short-term price predictors and self-exciting order flow. Guéant (2017) reconciles both formulations in one framework.
