What Is Market Making? How Market Makers Earn the Spread

Market makers post a price to buy and a price to sell at the same time, and in our worked example one books $4.00 of spread edge yet ends $16.00 down after a ten-cent fall. This guide shows why, and where automated market makers and the rules fit in.

Key takeaways

  • Market making is a business of risk: the spread is gross pay for carrying inventory and facing better-informed traders, not a guaranteed profit.
  • In the worked example below, a market maker books $4.00 of spread edge, then loses $20.00 on its remaining shares as the price falls ten cents.
  • “Maker” in maker-taker fees means any resting limit order, whoever places it.
  • Market makers are not required to trade at any price. Most obligations are quoting tests, though some designated market makers must also trade against the trend.
  • An automated market maker swaps quotes for a formula, and its signature cost is impermanent loss.

Market making is the practice of continuously quoting both a price to buy (the bid) and a price to sell (the ask) in the same instrument, so others can trade at once. The market maker earns the gap between them, the spread, and bears the risk that prices move against the inventory it accumulates.

What is market making, and why do markets need it?

Buyers and sellers do not always arrive at the same moment, especially in thinly traded stocks. A market maker fills the gap by standing ready on both sides, so you trade against its inventory instead of waiting for a counterparty.[1]

US securities law counts as a market maker any dealer that holds itself out as willing to buy and sell a security for its own account on a regular or continuous basis.[2] A dealer trades as a principal, while a broker is an agent that executes orders for others.[2]

What are the bid, ask and spread?

The bid is the best price a buyer currently offers, and the ask (or offer) is the best price a seller currently accepts. Their difference is the bid-ask spread, and the midpoint is the mid-price.[3] In our running example the bid is $49.99, the ask is $50.01, the spread is $0.02 and the mid-price is $50.00.

How a limit order book works: price-time priority and the queue

A limit order names a price and waits in the order book until an opposite order meets it. A market order executes at once at the best available price.[3] On an exchange, a market maker’s quotes are limit orders.

When orders rest at the same price, a common rule is price-time priority: best price first, then earliest arrival. CME says its first-in-first-out algorithm ranks orders by price and then time only, and an order loses its place if you increase its size or change its price, so queue position has value.[4] The rule is not universal: CME also runs pro-rata algorithms that split fills by size.[4]

A market making example: earning the spread, then an inventory loss

This toy example uses invented numbers and is not a strategy. The market maker buys at its bid, where takers sell into it, and sells at its ask, where takers buy from it. Profit and loss (P&L) is cash plus shares held times the current mid-price.

From the book in Figure 1, a taker first buys 400 shares with a market order: the trader ahead takes 300 and the market maker sells 100 at 50.01. Later a taker sells 700: the trader ahead takes 400 and the market maker buys 300 at 49.99. Then bad news arrives.

The +$4.00 is the market maker’s spread edge: each of the 400 shares it traded was one cent better than the 50.00 mid-price, and 400 × $0.01 = $4.00. Only $2.00 is locked in, from 100 shares bought at 49.99 and sold at 50.01; the other 200 are marked $2.00 above cost.

Then the mid-price falls to 49.90. The 200 shares lose 200 × $0.10 = $20.00 in value, while spread edge stays at +$4.00, so the result is −$16.00. A ten-cent move cost five times the spread edge: spread arrives a cent at a time, and inventory risk arrives in lumps.

How do market makers make money?

Mainly by buying at the bid and selling at the ask. The SEC’s 2010 description of passive market making names two sources of profit: earning the spread, and capturing the liquidity rebates trading centers pay to resting orders.[5]

Rebates come from maker-taker pricing, the predominant exchange fee structure in US stocks: the venue charges the order that takes liquidity and pays the order that provides it, typically funded by the access fee takers pay.[6] At Cboe BZX, for example, the standard rate from 19 October 2026 pays $0.0016 per share to add liquidity and charges $0.0030 to remove it for stocks at $1.00 or more; rates vary by tier.[7] On the 400 shares in our example the rebate would be $0.64, against $4.00 of spread edge.

Maker vs taker: what “maker” means

On US stock exchanges, a maker is any order that rests in the book and adds liquidity, and a taker is any order that executes against a resting order.[6] A retail investor’s resting limit order is a maker order,[3] and a market maker that crosses the spread to flatten a position is a taker.[5] A few venues invert the model and pay the taker.[3][6]

In the usual sense of payment for order flow, an off-exchange market maker pays a retail broker for the right to trade against its customers’ orders. Maker-taker rebates are a different arrangement, though a 2021 SEC staff report also calls exchange rebates “a form of payment for order flow” and notes that exchange pricing is posted publicly while wholesaler payments are negotiated individually.[3]

What eats a market maker’s profit? Adverse selection and inventory risk

Bank of England staff (Benos and Wetherilt, 2012) describe two main risks: trading against someone who knows more (adverse selection) and holding a position while the price moves (inventory risk).[1] Quotes are also standing offers, so a faster trader can pick off a stale one, which is why latency, the delay in updating quotes, matters.

Adverse selection: trading against better-informed traders

If a seller who expects a fall hits a market maker’s bid, the price tends to drop right afterwards, which the Bank of England article describes as forcing the dealer effectively to buy high and sell low.[1]

Example: A one-cent fall erases the half-spread

Say your bid at 49.99 is hit for 1,000 shares by a seller who knows the price is about to fall. Against the 50.00 mid-price you earned $10.00. If the mid-price falls one cent to 49.99, that edge is gone. At 49.95 you are down $40.00, and at 49.90 you are down $90.00.

Even a competitive market maker with zero expected profit needs a spread when some traders are informed. In a simplified illustration in the spirit of the Glosten-Milgrom model,[8] a stock is worth $99 or $101 with equal odds, one trader in five knows which, and the rest are equally likely to buy or sell.

A buy is 60% likely when the value is $101 and 40% likely when it is $99, so after a buy the value is $101 with probability 0.6. The break-even ask is 0.6 × $101 + 0.4 × $99 = $100.20, and by symmetry the bid is $99.80. The $0.40 spread equals the informed share (0.2) times the $2 gap in value. Estimates of the adverse-selection share of real spreads vary: 8 to 13 percent for small trades in a 1991 study of pre-decimal US data, against over 40 percent in earlier work, with order-processing costs the largest component.[9]

How do market makers set prices? Fair value, skew and hedging

A market maker quotes around an estimate of the asset’s true value, which the Avellaneda-Stoikov dealer model takes to be the mid-price. The gap between a quote and that value is its edge: wide enough to cover adverse selection, fees and inventory risk, narrow enough to be filled before rivals. Wider quotes earn more per fill but fill less often, and more informed traders or higher volatility push spreads out.[8][10] Firms can judge the edge by the price move after each fill, a markout; the SEC’s realized spread measures a similar idea.[5]

To manage inventory, market makers skew: they move both quotes in the direction that reduces the position, so a long market maker lowers both prices and its cheaper ask attracts buyers. One illustrative rule shifts both quotes down one cent for every 500 shares held. With no inventory the quotes are 49.99 and 50.01; at +1,000 shares they are 49.97 and 49.99, and in Figure 1’s book that 49.99 offer would hit the resting 49.99 bids at once, selling at the bid as a taker to shed shares. Firms also set position limits and may hedge the rest with an offsetting position in a related instrument, such as index futures.

The Avellaneda-Stoikov model formalizes the skew: given its inventory, the dealer computes a personal indifference price, usually called the reservation price, and quotes around it.[10] In the authors’ simulation (1,000 runs, risk aversion γ = 0.1, no informed traders), inventory-aware quotes averaged a profit of 65.0 with a standard deviation of 6.6, against 68.4 and 12.7 for symmetric quotes with the same average spread of 1.49. Skewing gave up about 5% of profit but nearly halved the standard deviation of profit and cut that of final inventory from 8.4 to 2.9 units.[10] The Avellaneda-Stoikov deep dive derives the formulas.

Types of market makers

Market makers differ in who appoints them and what they owe in return:

  • Designated market makers. A trading venue contracts them for particular securities, with quoting duties and incentives such as fee waivers or stipends.[1]
  • Electronic liquidity providers. In US equities, proprietary firms that post resting orders and earn spread and rebates, with no special privileges or obligations and free to enter and exit at will.[5][1] They overlap with high-frequency traders, though high-frequency trading is a technique: the SEC’s 2010 release lists passive market making as one of four strategy types it associated with high-frequency firms, with arbitrage, structural and directional ones.[5]
  • Off-exchange wholesalers. Firms that retail brokers send customer orders to. They may take the other side themselves or route the order elsewhere, pricing off public exchange quotes.[3]
  • Dealers. In 2012 the Bank of England described foreign exchange and many derivatives markets as relying almost exclusively on dealers.[1]

Automated market makers vs order-book market making

An automated market maker (AMM) replaces quotes with a formula. Liquidity providers deposit two tokens into a pool, and swaps execute against its reserves, so no counterparty order has to be waiting.[11] In a constant-product pool such as Uniswap v2, a decentralized exchange, reserves x and y must keep x × y = k, which stays constant or grows with every trade, so large trades move the price more.[11]

What is impermanent loss?

The signature cost is impermanent loss: the shortfall of the pool position against holding the same two tokens after their relative price changes. For a constant-product pool with equal value of each token and no fees, it depends only on r, the new price divided by the price at deposit.[12]

IL=2r1+r−1

Equation 1: Impermanent loss in a constant-product pool, without fees

In words: twice the square root of r, divided by one plus r, minus one. The result is negative because it is a shortfall: −5.7% at r = 2 and −20.0% at r = 4, and a halving costs the same as a doubling. The loss is “impermanent” because it vanishes if the price returns, and fees can offset it.

Beyond impermanent loss

A more recent view names the AMM counterpart of adverse selection “loss-versus-rebalancing”: the value lost to arbitrageurs who trade the pool back to the market price.[13] Concentrated-liquidity designs let providers confine capital to a price range; liquidity outside it is inactive and earns no fees,[14] and Equation 1 does not describe these positions. Providers also face risks this article does not cover, such as smart-contract risk. For arbitrage bots that trade against such pools on Solana, see Solana MEV bots explained.

Do market makers have to keep quoting? Obligations and rules

Only market makers with a formal role at a venue, such as a designation, registration or agreement, must keep quoting, and the rules set how much, often a set share of the trading day.[1] No rule reviewed here makes a market maker trade at any price, though a published quote must be honored up to its displayed size.[15] Most obligations test how often, how large and how close to the best price a firm quotes, with exceptions, but some designated market makers must also trade against the trend.

Designated market maker obligations at NYSE

NYSE Rule 104 requires a designated market maker (DMM) to assist in maintaining a fair and orderly market insofar as reasonably practicable, which NYSE said in 2023 includes trading for its own account when price continuity or depth is lacking.[16] Bank of England staff note that such duties can force a DMM to buy into a falling market at a loss.[1]

The same filing described a quoting test: hold a bid or an offer at the national best bid or offer (the best displayed price across US exchanges) for a minimum share of the trading day. The minimum is 15% for stocks averaging under a million shares a day, 10% for higher-volume stocks and 25% for exchange-traded products. Rules change, so read the current Rule 104.

EU rules for algorithmic market makers

As of 29 October 2026, MiFID II requires a firm that runs a market making strategy through algorithmic trading to provide liquidity regularly and predictably under a binding written agreement with the venue, except in exceptional circumstances. The technical standard RTS 8 sets the trigger: two-way quotes of comparable size at competitive prices for at least 50% of daily continuous trading hours on half the trading days of a month.[17]

US access fee cap and tick size: adopted, compliance not yet required

Under Regulation NMS, the SEC’s rulebook for US stock trading, Rule 610(c) caps the access fee, the fee for taking liquidity, at $0.003 per share for protected quotes priced at $1.00 or more. Rule 612 sets the minimum tick, the smallest price step, at one cent at those prices.[15] Protected quotes are an exchange’s best displayed bids and offers, which other venues generally may not trade through.

In September 2024 the SEC adopted amendments that cut the cap to $0.001 and set a half-cent minimum increment for stocks whose average quoted spread is 1.5 cents or less.[6] The electronic CFR already prints the amended text, but an SEC order of 11 June 2026 delayed compliance until the first business day of November 2027, the second extension.[15][18] The order does not restate the earlier limits, so that $0.003 and $0.01 still apply on 29 October 2026 is an inference. It fits venue practice, since Cboe BZX still charges $0.0030 to remove liquidity,[7] but check the current status of both rules.

Do market makers manipulate prices? Myths and spoofing

Ordinary market making is not manipulation, and its profit comes from the spread, not from betting against you: the SEC’s 2010 release describes passive market making as earning the spread and rebates.[5] A market maker loses money when it trades against better-informed flow, which is why off-exchange market makers try to tell retail orders apart and can offer them lower spreads.[3]

The sharper criticism is about conflicts of interest: SEC staff note that payments for retail order flow can create one for the retail broker.[3] Nor does a market maker always win: in a 2012 Bank of England sample of six liquid stocks, the volatile week brought larger losses as well as larger profits.[1]

Myth: heavy canceling means spoofing

The SEC’s 2010 description of passive market making says cancellation rates of 90% or more are possible and orders may live a second or less.[5] In US futures markets, spoofing means bidding or offering with the intent to cancel before execution; the CFTC reads the law as requiring intent beyond recklessness, with good-faith cancellations lawful.[19] Regulators can infer intent from patterns such as fill characteristics, so a high cancel rate is neither proof of spoofing nor a defense.

In US securities markets similar conduct is charged as layering: in an SEC case a jury found in 2019 that placing and canceling orders to trick others into trading was manipulation, and the Second Circuit affirmed in 2022.[20] EU market abuse rules use different wording and are not covered here.

Frequently asked questions

What is market making?

Market making is continuously quoting both a price to buy (the bid) and a price to sell (the ask) so that other traders can trade immediately. The market maker earns the spread between them and bears the risk that prices move against the inventory it accumulates. In US law a market maker includes any dealer that holds itself out as willing to buy and sell a security for its own account on a regular or continuous basis.

How do market makers make money?

Mainly by buying at the bid and selling at the ask, plus exchange rebates for adding liquidity and incentives paid to designated market makers. Those gains are reduced by adverse selection (trading against better-informed traders), inventory losses, fees and technology costs, so they are not guaranteed. In the worked example above a market maker books $4.00 of spread edge and then loses $20.00 on its remaining shares.

What is the difference between a market maker and a market taker?

In maker-taker pricing, a maker is any order that rests in the book and adds liquidity, and a taker is an order that executes against a resting order. On US stock exchanges makers usually earn a rebate and takers pay a fee, although a few venues invert this, and other markets, including crypto exchanges, set fees differently. The exchange pays any rebate to the member firm that routes the order, usually a broker, which may or may not pass it on to the investor. The labels describe orders, not firms: any trader can place a maker order or a taker order.

Do market makers have to buy and sell at any price?

No: a market maker must honor a quote it has published, up to the displayed size, but no rule reviewed here makes it trade at any price. Most obligations are specific tests, such as quoting at the best bid or offer for a minimum share of the trading day or within a maximum spread, and they come with exceptions. Some designated market makers also have a qualified duty to trade against the trend to keep prices orderly. MiFID II’s RTS 8 lists exceptional circumstances such as extreme volatility or a data-feed failure, and the Bank of England notes that designated market makers may have no option but to withdraw in extreme stress.

Is a market maker the same as a broker or a high-frequency trader?

No. A broker acts as an agent for clients, while a market maker is a dealer trading for its own account. High-frequency trading describes fast, high-volume proprietary trading, and the SEC’s 2010 concept release discussed passive market making as only one of four strategy types, so many fast traders are not market makers, although the two overlap.

Do market makers manipulate prices or bet against retail traders?

A market maker earns the spread on both sides rather than betting on direction, and it loses money when it trades against better-informed flow. Manipulation is illegal: in US futures, spoofing means bidding or offering with the intent to cancel before execution, and US securities regulators charge similar conduct as layering. Ordinary cancellations are not manipulation, and the SEC’s 2010 concept release notes that passive market making can involve cancellation rates of 90% or more.

What is adverse selection, and why does a spread exist?

Adverse selection means trading against someone who knows more than you do, so the price moves against you right after the trade. In a simplified Glosten-Milgrom style model a spread appears even with zero costs and zero expected profit, because the ask must reflect the value given that someone wants to buy. How much of real spreads this explains varies with the method: a 1991 study of pre-decimal US data put it at 8 to 13 percent for small trades, against over 40 percent in earlier work, and found order-processing costs the largest component.

How is an automated market maker different from a market maker?

An AMM replaces quotes with a pool of tokens and a formula such as x times y equals k, so traders swap against the pool instead of against posted orders. There are no quote decisions or price-time queues, and the main cost is impermanent loss: in a constant-product pool without fees, a doubling of the price leaves a liquidity provider about 5.7% behind holding the tokens. Concentrated-liquidity designs let providers choose a price range.
